World Classification Reference

Every world type the system view generates: the Orion's Arm Non-Luminary World Classification Scheme (NoLWoCS), plus the kinds of planet the exoplanet surveys actually find. Each entry gives the survey term for the same kind of world, where in a system it is most likely found (and where else it can turn up), and how often the generator makes it.

ScorchedEquilibrium temperature above about 1000 K: rock softens or melts, metals and silicates can be vapour.
HotInside the inner edge of the habitable zone. Water cannot stay liquid at the surface for long; it boils off or drives a runaway greenhouse.
Habitable zoneWhere a planet with an Earth-like atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface. Its inner part runs hot (Cytherean and Xeric types), its outer part cold (Tundral types).
CoolBetween the outer edge of the habitable zone and the snow line. Too cold for surface water, too warm for ices to have been abundant when the world formed.
IcyBeyond the snow line, where water ice (and further out, ammonia, methane and nitrogen ices) condensed in the planet-forming disc. Bodies here are rich in ice.
Reading the stripsSolid: where a world of that type is most likely found today. Faint: where one can also turn up after migrating, being scattered or being captured, and still keep its type. Empty: a world moved there would become something else: its atmosphere boiled away, its oceans frozen, its ices lost.

Asteroidal

Bodies too small to pull themselves round: asteroids, comets and small moons.

Jovian

Gas and ice giants, typed by temperature band. Neptunian means an ice giant (about 15 to 60 Earth masses); Jovian means a gas giant (above about 60). A giant's band follows its temperature wherever it ends up, so a giant that migrates simply changes band.

NoLWoCS

Shares a co-orbital configuration with another body: in the starmap, a planet set to share another planet's orbit at L4 or L5 (named like "Tau Ceti III-L4").

Science term
co-orbital planet; trojan planet
Examples
Saturn's moons Janus and Epimetheus (horseshoe orbit).

Promethean

Setting

Tidally locked 1:1: the world turns once per orbit, keeping one face to its star (or a moon to its planet) forever. A permanent dayside, nightside and twilight ring. Named for Prometheus, chained in place under the sun. Not part of the original scheme: this setting's own term.

Science term
tidally locked (synchronous rotation); "eyeball" planet
Examples
Earth's Moon; most planets close to red dwarfs, such as Proxima Centauri b.

Ixionian

Setting

Tidally locked into a spin-orbit resonance other than 1:1, usually because its orbit is eccentric: it turns a fixed whole number of times for every few orbits (3:2, 2:1). Days are long and the same longitudes face the star at every closest approach. Named for Ixion, bound to an ever-turning wheel of fire. Not part of the original scheme: this setting's own term.

Science term
spin-orbit resonance (non-synchronous tidal lock)
Examples
Mercury (3:2: three turns every two orbits, a solar day of 176 Earth days).

If a world moves

Migration, scattering and capture can leave a world stably orbiting where its type cannot last. Giants simply take the temperature band of their new orbit, and a rogue planet captured into orbit takes whatever type its mass and new zone dictate.

How worlds age

Types are not permanent. Stars brighten through their lives, then swell into red giants and shrink to white dwarfs; planets lose heat, air and water. These are the common paths, left to right in time.

Earth-like world, Sun-like star, habitable zone

  1. EoGaianfirst ~0.5 Gyr
  2. MesoGaian~0.5–2 Gyr: microbial life
  3. EuGaian~2–5 Gyr: oxygen, complex life
  4. PostGaianstar ~10% brighter (~1 Gyr from Earth now)
  5. Xericoceans retreating
  6. Cythereanoceans gone, runaway greenhouse
  7. Hermianred giant: stripped or swallowed

The Sun brightens about 10% per billion years, so every habitable-zone world drifts toward the hot edge.

Venus path: hot side of the habitable zone

  1. Pelagicyoung, possibly wet
  2. PelaCythereanmoist greenhouse
  3. Cythereanoceans lost

Venus may have had oceans for its first few hundred million years.

Mars path: small world, habitable zone or just beyond

  1. EoAreanyoung, thick air, water
  2. AreanLacustriclakes and seas
  3. EuAreancore cools, magnetic field fails, air escapes
  4. AreanTundralwater locked as ground ice
  5. AreanXericstar brightens

Small worlds lose their atmospheres fastest: low gravity, and their cores cool and stop shielding them sooner.

Cold world at the outer edge of the habitable zone

  1. GaianTundralice age at formation
  2. EoGaianstar brightens: thaw
  3. EuGaianthen as the Earth-like path

The habitable zone moves outward as the star brightens, sweeping over worlds that formed too cold.

Red dwarf habitable zone

  1. PelaCythereanstar's bright youth (up to ~1 Gyr): water boiled off
  2. HyperXericdesiccated survivor
  3. EuAreanflares strip the air
  4. Vesperianlocked face to the star

Red dwarfs are far brighter in their first few hundred million years, then flare for billions more. Worlds that keep enough water can stay Pelagic or Vesperian for tens of billions of years.

Sub-Neptune close to its star

  1. Super-puffyoung, puffy envelope
  2. Gas dwarfsettled
  3. Nebuloushydrogen escapes, helium stays
  4. SuperHermianenvelope gone: the "radius valley"
  5. Pyrothalassicif the core is still molten

Starlight boils away light gases. Survey statistics show the gap between sub-Neptunes and stripped super-Earths that this leaves.

Hot Jupiter

  1. Pyro-Jovianmigrates inward
  2. Cali-Jovianclosest orbits: inflated, evaporating
  3. Chthonianenvelope gone

Only the closest, lightest giants lose their whole envelope; most hot Jupiters keep theirs for the star's lifetime.

Giant planet cooling

  1. Zesto-Jovianfirst ~100 Myr, glowing from formation heat
  2. Psychro-Joviancools over billions of years
  3. Cryo-Jovianfar out, very old

A giant's band reflects its own heat as well as its star's. Young giants are warmer than their orbit alone would make them.

Tidally heated moon

  1. Hephaestianeccentric orbit, strong tides
  2. Europantides weaken as the orbit circularises
  3. LithicGelidianheat runs out, ocean freezes

Resonances between moons can keep tidal heating going for billions of years (Io, Europa and Ganymede).

Icy world when its star becomes a red giant

  1. Ymirianmain sequence
  2. BathyPelagicred giant: the habitable zone sweeps outward
  3. Titanianthick atmosphere from thawed ices
  4. PelaGelidicwhite dwarf: refreezes

A red giant warms the outer system for a few hundred million years: a late, temporary habitable zone among the ice worlds.

Survivor around a white dwarf

  1. Hermianinner world after the red giant
  2. Selenianwhite dwarf cools
  3. Vesperiana water-rich world scattered close in

A white dwarf's habitable zone is tiny (around 0.01 AU) and shrinks as it cools. Worlds there are tidally locked.
